## The Opposite of the White Bear Principle: Unintentional Recall

The White Bear Principle, also known as the ironic process theory, describes the phenomenon where actively trying to suppress a thought ends up making it more prominent and persistent. 

However, the opposite of this phenomenon, where something unintentionally comes to mind more easily the less you try to recall it, doesn't have a single, universally accepted term. However, here are a few options that capture different aspects of the experience:

**1. Spontaneous Recall:** This term emphasizes the unexpected and effortless nature of remembering something without actively trying.

**2. Incidental Learning:** This term highlights how information can be absorbed and retained without conscious effort, often through repeated exposure or association with other memories.

**3. Cryptomnesia:** This term describes the phenomenon of unconsciously plagiarizing one's own work, where a previously forgotten idea or memory resurfaces and is mistaken for something new.

**4. Latent Memory:** This term refers to memories that are not consciously accessible but can still influence thoughts and behaviors. These memories can sometimes be retrieved through indirect means, such as through association with other cues.

**5. The Zeigarnik Effect:** This effect describes how unfinished tasks or unresolved issues tend to be remembered more easily than completed ones. This could be relevant if the information you're trying to remember is connected to an unfinished task.

**6. The Spacing Effect:** This effect describes how information is better remembered when it is learned and reviewed over spaced intervals rather than all at once. This suggests that taking breaks from trying to recall something can actually be beneficial.

**7. The Tip-of-the-Tongue (TOT) Phenomenon:** This is the feeling of knowing something but being unable to recall it at the moment. This experience can be frustrating, but it often resolves itself spontaneously when you stop actively trying to remember.

Ultimately, the specific term you choose will depend on the context and the specific nature of the experience you're trying to describe. 

It's also worth noting that these terms are not mutually exclusive, and the forgetting and remembering process can often involve elements of multiple mechanisms.
