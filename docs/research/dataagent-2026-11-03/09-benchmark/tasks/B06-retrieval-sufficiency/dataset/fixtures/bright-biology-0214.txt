## The Disappearing Yeast: A Kitchen Chemistry Mystery

### 1. The Problem: Solid to Liquid Transformation

The experiment you described presents an intriguing puzzle: how can solid yeast and sugar seemingly dissolve into a liquid within minutes? To understand this phenomenon, let's break down the process step by step.

### 2. Step-by-Step Reasoning

**a) Yeast: A Living Organism:**

- Baker's yeast, unlike dry yeast, is a living organism composed of single-celled fungi called Saccharomyces cerevisiae. These cells have a semi-permeable membrane that allows the passage of certain molecules.

**b) Sugar: The Fuel Source:**

- Sugar, in this case, acts as a readily available food source for the yeast. When it comes into contact with the yeast cells, it triggers a series of chemical reactions.

**c) The Power of Osmosis:**

- The key to understanding the "disappearing" act lies in a process called osmosis. Due to the concentration difference, water from the surrounding environment (including the moisture within the yeast cells) moves across the semi-permeable membrane into the sugar solution. This influx of water causes the yeast cells to swell and eventually burst, releasing their internal contents.

**d) Enzymes and Chemical Reactions:**

- The released yeast enzymes, along with the dissolved sugar, participate in a series of biochemical reactions, primarily fermentation. During fermentation, the yeast breaks down the sugar molecules into simpler compounds, releasing carbon dioxide (CO2) as a byproduct.

**e) The Liquid Result:**

- The combination of the influx of water, the breakdown of sugar, and the release of CO2 results in a significant increase in the volume of the mixture. This, coupled with the liquefaction of the yeast cells due to their bursting, creates the illusion of both yeast and sugar dissolving into a liquid.
