## The Actor-Observer Bias

The phenomenon you're describing is called the **actor-observer bias**. It's a cognitive bias that leads people to attribute their own actions to external factors (situational causes), while attributing the actions of others to internal factors (dispositional causes). This bias can lead to a double standard in how we judge ourselves and others.

Here's how the bias plays out in the examples you provided:

* **He lied:** This statement implies that the person sees lying as an inherent character flaw in the other person.
* **You changed your mind:** This statement suggests that the person attributes their change of mind to external factors, such as new information or circumstances.
* **I reconsidered my decision:** This statement further softens the self-judgment, suggesting thoughtful deliberation rather than a simple change of mind.

The actor-observer bias can be explained by a few factors:

* **Perspective:** We have access to our own internal thoughts and feelings, while we can only observe the external behavior of others. This difference in perspective makes it easier to attribute our own actions to external factors.
* **Self-serving bias:** We tend to want to see ourselves in a positive light, and attributing our actions to external factors helps us maintain a positive self-image.
* **Fundamental attribution error:** We have a tendency to overestimate the role of personality traits in explaining other people's behavior, while underestimating the role of situational factors.

The actor-observer bias can have a significant impact on our relationships and interactions with others. It can lead to misunderstandings, conflict, and even prejudice. By being aware of this bias, we can learn to be more understanding and forgiving of others, and to hold ourselves to the same standards that we hold others to.

Here are some additional thoughts on the actor-observer bias:

* The bias is not always negative. It can sometimes be helpful, for example, by allowing us to maintain a positive self-image in the face of setbacks.
* The bias can be mitigated by perspective-taking exercises, such as trying to see things from the other person's point of view.
* The bias is less pronounced in people who are high in empathy and perspective-taking ability.
